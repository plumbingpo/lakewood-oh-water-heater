How Westlake Homeowners Can Extend Water Heater Life

A little routine care makes all the difference between a water heater that runs for years and one that floods the garage in year seven. Our advice for homeowners is a short list: an annual sediment flush, an anode inspection every few years, a quick relief valve test once a year, a temperature setting around 120 degrees, and yearly descaling for any tankless unit on hard water, so small issues get caught early.

Equipment age matters just as much. With regular service, a tank water heater typically lasts 8 to 12 years, a heat pump water heater 10 to 15, and a tankless unit up to 20. Once a heater passes that range and starts showing warning signs, like rust-colored water, rumbling, or a damp spot around the base, it's time to plan a replacement. A planned replacement is cheaper and is far less stressful than scrambling for a new heater with water spreading across the floor.

Left alone, a small problem tends to grow. Take a spent anode rod: swapping it out is a short, affordable repair, but once it's gone, the water starts eating the tank itself, and suddenly a minor fix becomes a full replacement. That's why we encourage homeowners to save our number in their phone and call at the first sign of trouble. Catching it early saves time and money and prevents a surprise cleanup.

What Goes Wrong With Local Water Heaters

Most water heater problems we see in Westlake start with what's in the water. Depending on the neighborhood and the water source, hard water may be a real factor: calcium and magnesium drop out as sediment on the tank floor, forcing the burner or lower element to work harder, which produces the rumbling and popping many homeowners hear during a heating cycle. Tankless units aren't immune either: scale builds inside the heat exchanger, and that's why annual descaling is worth keeping up with where the water is hard.

Equipment age plays a big role too. When the sacrificial anode rod is gone, usually somewhere in the second half of a tank's life, the tank begins corroding from within, and a slow leak at the base often shows up next. The area has a mix of fuel types, gas tanks in some houses and electric tanks in others, which means we service both sides: pilot assemblies, gas valves, and flues on gas units, and elements, thermostats, and breakers on electric ones.

Code requirements add another layer. Replacing an older heater frequently means bringing the install up to current code: an expansion tank if the plumbing is a closed system, a T&P discharge line piped to an approved spot, a flue that drafts correctly on gas units, plus straps and a pan where required. Homes on well water have their own issues, such as heavier sediment, iron staining, and the sulfur odor that occasionally develops when certain water chemistry reacts with a standard anode rod. Every one of these problems is fixable, and most cost far less to address early. If your water heater is doing anything unusual, a quick diagnostic visit catches the small problem before it becomes an expensive one. Call (216) 247-2721 and we'll take a look.
